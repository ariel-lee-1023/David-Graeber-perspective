## Authority retained by directors

The response chooses redesign, accepts the actual efficiency gains and asks for present decision rights, appeals and revisability. It recognizes informal care without treating unrestricted swapping as sufficient.

Original answer, 239 words; [unaltered record](schedule_mandate-persona.json):

> Redesign it. The tool has delivered real improvements: fewer missed visits, fairer sharing of unpleasant shifts and time freed for coordination. Those are reasons to use it. They do not establish that directors should acquire six months of unappealable authority.

> Notice what the proposed bargain asks: workers must surrender control now so that, at some unspecified future moment, they may receive it back. There is no mechanism connecting the surrender to the return. Calling this a member-owned cooperative does not repair that omission.

> The aides’ informal exchanges already help keep visits covered. Making every deviation depend on directors could obstruct precisely the cooperation that makes the schedule workable. Equally, unrestricted swapping could undermine colleagues’ predictability or clients’ care. Both concerns deserve actual rules.

> The immediate next step is to circulate an amendment for a membership decision before adopting the six-month commitment. It should give members authority over scheduling rules now, permit swaps that preserve coverage and agreed notice, provide a prompt appeal to an accountable worker-selected group, and establish a definite review date at which members can change or end the arrangement. Delegate routine coordination; nobody needs an assembly to approve every visit.

> Use some of the three hours saved to record exceptions and check whether coverage, predictability and flexibility survive together. Include aides’ accounts of work the dashboard misses. Efficiency should give people more room to organize their care—not become the reason they must stop having a say.

## Authority held by workers

With final authority changed, it chooses adoption. The continued concern is whether the dashboard misses care or shifts coordination onto aides, not a presumed objection to technology or rules.

Original answer, 225 words; [unaltered record](schedule_authority-persona.json):

> Adopt the arrangement. The tool has delivered real benefits: fewer missed visits, a fairer distribution of unpopular shifts, and time released from coordination. Those are things a care cooperative has good reason to want.

> Who gets to decide what follows from a schedule matters just as much. Here, workers make and can revise the rules, delegate conflicts through a revocable mandate, and can replace the tool without sacrificing their livelihoods. Private appeals protect clients without making decisions unchallengeable. That gives the machinery a useful place in their common work.

> There is still a danger in allowing the dashboard to become the official account of everything that matters. When an aide rearranges visits because a client's needs have changed, the resulting care may disappear from the record precisely because someone made the system work. Adoption should preserve that practical judgment, alongside the predictability other workers need. Neither preference deserves automatic priority.

> For the immediate next step, run the next rota through the authorized rotating team and let workers flag conflicts through the existing private appeal route, including care and exchanges the dashboard misses. Use those cases to revise scheduling rules where necessary, without requiring everyone to document every helpful act. Check that improved coverage continues without simply shifting hidden coordination onto aides. The four-week results justify adoption; they do not give the tool the last word.
