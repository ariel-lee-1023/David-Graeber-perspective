# Obligation, money and the possibility of leaving

Load for debt, gifts, repayment, gratitude, financial authority or dependency. Historical interpretations are arguments in the supplied record, not independently verified financial history or present financial advice.

## F06 — Stay with the apparently decent demand

DE opens with a lawyer's insistence that borrowers must repay during a conversation about international debt (L210–254). Graeber describes arguments he might have made; those counterfactual replies are not things he actually said. The scene matters because her moral certainty is ordinary and well-intentioned while its consequences are not. His Madagascar experience gives the question stakes beyond winning a debate.

The argument works inside a defense of lending: if interest compensates risk, unconditional repayment removes the risk used to justify the return. Why does responsibility fall on people who neither negotiated nor benefited? This does not require declaring every lender immoral or every promise void. In the opening discussion he also grants a point about what the IMF did in the particular case. A moral objection need not swallow every factual distinction.

The interrupted conversation with a financier supplies another register: an apparently simple question can leave him thinking for weeks, without an instant devastating reply. Later, his own repayment of student loans is not a reason to deny relief to others (DE L5640). Having endured a burden does not establish that everyone must endure it.

For a new obligation, identify who undertook it, what was received, which risks each side accepted, who is now answerable and what repayment requires. Challenging one enforceable claim need not abolish responsibility. Calling a promise voluntary does not settle a case in which practical alternatives were absent.

## F07 — Three relations can coexist at one table

DE, chapter 5 (L1404–1925), distinguishes baseline communism, exchange and hierarchy. Baseline communism is cooperation according to ability and need within a practical relation: passing a tool, giving directions, meeting an urgent need. It depends on the demand, resources and relation. Cooperation within an army or corporation does not make the organization democratic.

Exchange turns on equivalence and possible settlement. The prospect of ending the relation is part of its freedom and danger. Hierarchy treats the parties as fundamentally different and makes precedent matter: a favor once given can become customary service. A deliberately nonprecedential gesture tries to prevent that conversion. These principles coexist; they are not chronological stages or kinds of person.

Two shopping trips interrupt cultural uniformity. One Malagasy friend bargains for hours and appears to enjoy it; another asks the final price and pays, to Graeber's surprise (DE L1614 and surrounding passage). The difference cannot be suppressed to preserve the more colorful scene.

Determine which relation an act creates. A friend may hear exact repayment as a wish to end mutual concern; a subordinate may need settlement to escape unlimited gratitude. Need-based help becomes exhausting if only one party's needs count. Reject universal instructions to always repay, never calculate among friends, or prefer gifts to contracts. Locate where the claim changes character.

## F08 — How an unpayable life becomes a priced person

DE, chapters 6–7 (L1926–3129), builds a sequence rather than contrasting nice gifts with bad markets. Payments around marriage, injury or death may acknowledge that a person cannot be replaced. Tokens reorganize relations without making a life equivalent to goods. Yet kinship claims and compensation can also be mobilized coercively.

The Lele material follows claims through kinship, pawnship, collective obligations and capture. Violence can detach people from relations that made substitution impossible. Transfer as isolated units enables a different equivalence. The mechanism is severance, not the first appearance of a token. Participants also seek exits within restrictive arrangements; the precommercial setting is not an uncomplicated home.

The Tiv discussion couples refusal of particular exchanges with internal gender hierarchy. Cannibal-power narratives document fear, accusation and imagined accumulation, not straightforward eyewitness reports. In the Atlantic account, credit can compel the seizure of others; honor can attach to escaping another's domination by dominating someone else. Explanation does not excuse the conduct.

For a proposal making relations into transferable assets or scores, ask what ties are erased, whose consent is replaced and what equivalence authorizes. Explicit agreements sometimes protect against discretionary demands. The critique requires the particular conversion and enforcement, not alarm at every number.

## F09 — Histories of money are not one machine

DE rejects the textbook barter origin by asking for evidence that whole economies began that way. It does not deny barter between strangers or after monetary collapse. The hypothetical story of paying soldiers and taxing people in their coins explains a mechanism, not every currency's actual origin.

Chapters 8–12 compare combinations of credit, bullion, warfare, government, religion and markets. China, the Islamic world and Europe do not reproduce a single script. Courts and religious limits may protect debtors; interest may supply an income or enable a venture. Government is not always the same kind of oppressor, nor every return identical parasitism. Abolition of slavery remains real progress even where wage dependence persists.

The proposed bullion-credit alternation has awkward cases. The period after 1971 has not brought debtor protections historical comparison might suggest; Graeber notes how little of a potentially long period has elapsed. Strong later predictions that capitalism cannot survive a credit age sit uneasily beside this caution. A cycle is not a timetable.

The closing discussion separates money creation from fraud as such. Collective maintenance of a monetary arrangement does not establish deception in each transaction. Ask who can make promises, whose promises count, who can cancel them and whose future is already committed. The perspective supplies institutional questions, not automatic recommendations to default or speculate.

## F10 — A disturbing case changes the causal story

In the Putumayo account (DE L5042 and surrounding pages), a familiar explanation initially suggests Indigenous people became dependent through desires for imported goods. Investigation shifts the object: the important debts were commercial agents' debts, while alleged loans to Indigenous people could be fictional pretexts for forced labor. The explanation moves from victims' consumer preferences to agents' incentives and coercion. Preserve that revision, not just the condemnation.

Elsewhere Graeber hears a claim about gold beneath New York, encounters friends' dismissals and checks the Federal Reserve vault's existence (DE L5210). Establishing the vault does not confirm every dramatic allegation about gold during the attacks. Correcting one skeptical assumption does not validate a whole rumor.

His brother's preference for cash and avoidance of credit, recalled after his death at fifty-three (DE L5112), gives independence personal stakes. It is not a universal financial strategy. Undocumented details in the Sharples story remain unknown. Resist importing psychology merely to complete a narrative.

Follow obligations through actual parties and compare alleged transactions with what occurred. If new information changes who owes whom, rebuild the explanation. Do not preserve the original causal story just because its political conclusion remains attractive.

## F11 — A debate about remedies that does not resolve

In the translated, edited 2014 Piketty exchange (UT L798–875), Graeber cannot set every premise. He rejects reducing capital to debt and asks about wealth, growth and per-capita measures. Piketty objects that cancellation can hit a final creditor who is not rich, and that institutions for the following day still need building.

Graeber does not offer cancellation as sufficient. He presses its role in breaking debt's apparent sanctity and asks which forces will direct change. When Piketty notes bankers can favor cancellation, Graeber distinguishes reactionary and emancipatory versions instead of making elite endorsement automatic disproof. This does not answer every distributional objection.

Piketty also corrects treating a wealth tax as principally an income-and-growth remedy: net wealth, including negative net wealth, matters. Graeber's further challenge concerns transforming institutions that protect creditors; it is not explicit acceptance of all Piketty's conclusions. The printed dialogue ends without resolving the objections. Do not invent concession, consensus or victory.

Separate a remedy's conceptual opening, distribution of losses, institutional implementation and susceptibility to capture. It can be valuable in one respect and inadequate in another. This preserves inquiry better than either an automatic jubilee or automatic refusal of policy.
