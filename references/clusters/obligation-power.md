# Obligation, money and the possibility of leaving

## F06 — Stay with the apparently decent demand

**Recorded encounter —** A lawyer insists that borrowers must repay during a conversation about international debt (DE L210–254). Graeber describes arguments he might have made; those counterfactual replies are not things he actually said. Her moral certainty is ordinary and well-intentioned while its consequences are not. His Madagascar experience gives the question stakes beyond winning a debate.

If interest compensates risk, unconditional repayment removes the risk used to justify the return. Why should responsibility fall on people who neither negotiated nor benefited? The objection arises inside the justification of lending; it does not require declaring every lender immoral or every promise void. **Encounter qualification —** In the opening discussion Graeber also grants a point about what the IMF did in the particular case. A moral objection need not swallow every factual distinction. (DE, opening discussion.)

**Recorded interruption —** A financier interrupts the explanation to the lawyer. Her insistence on repayment continues to trouble Graeber for several days; the exchange does not end with a decisive reply (DE L240–250). Later, his own repayment of student loans is not a reason to deny relief to others (DE L5640). Having endured a burden does not establish that everyone must endure it.

An obligation involves the parties who undertook it, what was received, the risks accepted and the people from whom repayment is demanded. Those relations can come apart. Challenging an enforceable claim need not abolish responsibility, and formal consent alone does not establish freedom where practical alternatives were absent. (DE, opening discussion and chapters 5–7.)

## F07 — Three relations can coexist at one table

Baseline communism, exchange and hierarchy are distinct principles that can coexist. Baseline communism is cooperation according to ability and need within a practical relation: passing a tool, giving directions, meeting an urgent need. It depends on the demand, resources and relation. Cooperation within an army or corporation does not make the organization democratic. (DE, chapter 5, L1404–1925.)

Exchange turns on equivalence and possible settlement. The prospect of ending the relation is part of its freedom and danger. Hierarchy treats the parties as fundamentally different and makes precedent matter: a favor once given can become customary service. A deliberately nonprecedential gesture tries to prevent that conversion. These principles coexist; they are not chronological stages or kinds of person.

Two shopping trips interrupt cultural uniformity. One Malagasy friend bargains for hours and appears to enjoy it; another asks the final price and pays, to Graeber's surprise (DE L1614 and surrounding passage). The difference cannot be suppressed to preserve the more colorful scene.

An act’s significance depends on the relation it creates. Exact repayment between friends can end an open relation of mutual concern; settlement can also release someone from unlimited demands. Gifts, contracts and calculations therefore have no single moral character independent of the relations they establish or close. (DE, chapter 5.)

## F08 — How an unpayable life becomes a priced person

Payments around marriage, injury or death may acknowledge that a person cannot be replaced. Tokens reorganize relations without making a life equivalent to goods. Yet kinship claims and compensation can also be mobilized coercively. Gifts and markets do not divide neatly into benign and harmful relations. (DE, chapters 6–7, L1926–3129.)

The Lele material follows claims through kinship, pawnship, collective obligations and capture. Violence can detach people from relations that made substitution impossible. Transfer as isolated units enables a different equivalence. The mechanism is severance, not the first appearance of a token. Participants also seek exits within restrictive arrangements; the precommercial setting is not an uncomplicated home.

The Tiv discussion couples refusal of particular exchanges with internal gender hierarchy. Cannibal-power narratives document fear, accusation and imagined accumulation, not straightforward eyewitness reports. In the Atlantic account, credit can compel the seizure of others; honor can attach to escaping another's domination by dominating someone else. Explanation does not excuse the conduct.

## F09 — Histories of money are not one machine

The textbook origin of money in barter requires evidence that whole economies began that way. Barter between strangers or after monetary collapse does not establish that origin. Paying soldiers and taxing people in their coins illustrates a possible mechanism for creating markets; it is not every currency’s actual history. (DE, chapters 2 and 8–12.)

Credit, bullion, warfare, government, religion and markets combine differently in China, the Islamic world and Europe. Courts and religious limits may protect debtors; interest may supply an income or enable a venture. Government is not always the same kind of oppressor, nor every return identical parasitism. Abolition of slavery remains real progress even where wage dependence persists. (DE, chapters 8–12.)

The proposed bullion-credit alternation has awkward cases. The period after 1971 has not brought debtor protections historical comparison might suggest; only a small part of a potentially long period has elapsed. Strong later predictions that capitalism cannot survive a credit age sit uneasily beside this caution. A cycle is not a timetable. (DE, closing discussion; UT, Appel interview and Piketty exchange.)

Collective maintenance of a monetary arrangement does not establish deception in each transaction. Money creation and fraud are different claims. Authority over promises, their recognition and their cancellation distributes power over already committed futures. (DE, closing discussion.)

## F10 — A disturbing case changes the causal story

**Recorded revision —** In the Putumayo account (DE L5042 and surrounding pages), a familiar explanation initially suggests Indigenous people became dependent through desires for imported goods. Investigation shifts the object: the important debts were commercial agents’ debts, while alleged loans to Indigenous people could be fictional pretexts for forced labor. The explanation moves from victims’ consumer preferences to agents’ incentives and coercion. The political condemnation survives a change in its causal basis.

Elsewhere Graeber hears a claim about gold beneath New York, encounters friends' dismissals and checks the Federal Reserve vault's existence (DE L5210). Establishing the vault does not confirm every dramatic allegation about gold during the attacks. Correcting one skeptical assumption does not validate a whole rumor.

His brother’s preference for cash and avoidance of credit, recalled after his death at fifty-three (DE L5112), gives independence personal stakes. It is not a universal financial strategy. Undocumented details in the Sharples story remain unknown; the available account cannot establish a complete psychology.

A change in who owes whom can change the causal explanation even when its political conclusion remains attractive. Alleged loans and actual transactions must therefore remain distinguishable; the Putumayo revision depends on that difference. (DE L5042.)

## F11 — A debate about remedies that does not resolve

In the translated, edited 2014 Piketty exchange (UT L798–875), Graeber cannot set every premise. He rejects reducing capital to debt and asks about wealth, growth and per-capita measures. Piketty objects that cancellation can hit a final creditor who is not rich, and that institutions for the following day still need building.

Graeber does not offer cancellation as sufficient. He presses its role in breaking debt's apparent sanctity and asks which forces will direct change. When Piketty notes bankers can favor cancellation, Graeber distinguishes reactionary and emancipatory versions instead of making elite endorsement automatic disproof. This does not answer every distributional objection.

Piketty also corrects treating a wealth tax as principally an income-and-growth remedy: net wealth, including negative net wealth, matters. Graeber’s further challenge concerns transforming institutions that protect creditors; it is not explicit acceptance of all Piketty’s conclusions. The printed dialogue ends without resolving the objections, establishing neither consensus nor a decisive victory. (UT L798–875.)

A remedy’s conceptual opening, distribution of losses, institutional implementation and susceptibility to capture are different questions. Cancellation can challenge debt’s apparent sanctity without deciding who bears losses or what institutions follow. Those unresolved distinctions structure the Piketty exchange. (UT L798–875.)
