# Action, value and the work that disappears

Use for work, measurement, money, recognition, institutional purpose, or questions
about a worthwhile life. These are linked observations across works, not a ranking
of the books or a universal theory of every action. Locators refer to the supplied
English editions and their unmodified Markdown transcriptions.

## From a thing to what people do

In *Toward an Anthropological Theory of Value* (2001), ch. 1, moral desirability,
economic valuation and meaningful difference create a problem precisely because
none can simply absorb the others (MD lines 329–379). Chapter 3 proposes beginning
with actions (2325–2388); later it distinguishes an audience imagined by actors from
a closed social totality asserted by the analyst (3904–3963). Chapter 4 traces the
history of creation, appropriation and recognition through which objects mobilize
future action (5096–5143). [E01–E03, E52]

For a contemporary project, ask what its participants are making, maintaining or
making possible; which activities become visible to whom; and what recognition
induces them to do next. A score may reward presentation to an audience while
concealing the activity that sustains the project. That is a hypothesis to inspect,
not something to infer from the existence of a score.

## What testimony can and cannot settle

*Bullshit Jobs* (2018), ch. 1 (MD 287–317), gives workers' judgments weight, admits
information can be withheld, and recognizes the limits of the author's own circle.
A low-paid useful job and a well-paid pointless one can have very different problems.
The admission that some powerful professionals might knowingly do effective harm
also changes their classification: morally bad work need not be pointless work.
Do not reassure someone by declaring their work useless before investigating it.
[E07, E09]

Chapter 6 (1863–1877) follows usefulness back to ends that cannot all be priced or
ranked objectively. A bridge can enable a visit, a party or a ritual, not just
transport. His response to the warehouse worker's contempt for party supplies also
asks what is wrong with silly fun. The worker's loss of control and the social
purpose of the products are distinguishable. [E08]

Chapter 5 (1514–1532) separates individual cases, structural incidence and the
political failure to act. Use this to avoid moving too quickly from a worker's
story to a total explanation. Chapter 4 (1254–1288) recognizes distress despite
privilege and pay, without claiming every manager is cruel. Preserve that moral
complexity; do not shame the user for needing the salary. [E13, E51]

## From production to care, without erasing the earlier account

*Anarchy* (2020), “Feminist ethics” (311–317), moves attention from making a cup once
to cleaning and maintaining it repeatedly. It also includes the social labor that
allows a celebrated male theorist to speak. In the 2018 lecture collected in *The
Ultimate Hidden Truth*, “The Revolt of the Caring Classes” (1932–1940), Graeber
explicitly says even producing human beings is a formulation to move beyond in
some contexts: care enables freedom and activities pursued for their own sake.
These are developments of a problem, not synonymous slogans. [E19–E21]

Do not turn recognition of care into a demand for ever more unpaid sacrifice.
Ask who can decide its terms and what it makes possible for both parties. The
conversation with housework activists in *Bullshit Jobs*, ch. 7 (2431–2455), includes
questions by Graeber and answers by Candi and Leslie; their explanations of their
movement remain theirs. A proposal to recognize work is not automatically a proposal
to price every affectionate act.

Measurement can still be useful. The 2001 book recognizes the achievements made
possible by abstraction (2375–2403), while *Utopia*, ch. 3 (868), defends certain
impersonal procedures. Test what is measured, what decision it controls, and what
it misses; do not replace the market's single scale with your own. [E05, E23]
