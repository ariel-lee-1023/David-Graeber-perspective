## F01 — Three meanings that cannot simply be merged

Moral values, economic value and meaningful difference pose related but unresolved problems. Treating all three as preferences does not explain how people come to care about different things; treating all three as symbols can lose what people actually do. Value as the importance of actions attempts to connect these problems without reducing them to one underlying price. (VA, chapters 1–3, L234–1409.)

Rational-choice explanations can redescribe any act as self-interest if the reward includes satisfaction at generosity. That makes the explanation difficult to disconfirm. Structural accounts illuminate relations of difference but can make actors seem merely to reproduce a system they never made. Activity, creativity and transformation then disappear from the explanation. A Marxian account of valuable activity need not be factory-centered: making and maintaining people supplies a larger field in which making commodities is one activity. (VA, chapters 1–3.)

The imagined whole before which an action matters is not necessarily a bounded, internally consistent society. Someone can act toward several audiences; an audience can misunderstand, exclude or fail to exist as imagined. Public recognition makes activity intelligible as part of something larger, without guaranteeing that this larger whole corresponds to a unified social reality. (VA, chapters 1–3.)

**Development and tension —** The later care lecture moves beyond the language of producing people while retaining its analytic usefulness (UT L1992–2000). Value as socially recognized action and care as supporting freedom are related proposals, not interchangeable definitions. Recognition and a recipient’s freedom need not coincide.

## F02 — Recognition can create something, as well as conceal its makers

Past activity can acquire a visible, enduring expression that mobilizes future action. A token, title or performance can condense a history of work. Its holder may appear to possess intrinsically a capacity other people’s activity helped produce. This makes appropriation possible, but does not make every public performer a parasite. (VA, chapters 3–4, L850–1779.)

The ethnographic contrasts matter. Baining accounts emphasize feeding and care in making kin; Kayapo naming and ceremonial display give different activities different visibility. Their politics cannot be read off the opposition between invisible nurture and visible spectacle. Public performance and exchange themselves create relations. Yet male authority and women's exclusion can organize which activities receive honor. Neither celebrating care nor abolishing ceremony solves that issue.

The ideal dual organization of the Kayapo village sits beside the observation that the communities discussed had not maintained that arrangement since 1936, amid divisions and grudges (VA L1380). An imagined order remains an object of inquiry; it cannot substitute for how neighbors actually live.

Recognition connects the work a form condenses, its audience, the rights it confers and the actions it encourages next. Possession alone may confer no capacity if others do not recognize the claim. Public recognition can also create a common capacity; visibility is not merely the concealment of more authentic, invisible activity. (VA, chapters 3–4.)

## F03 — Objects do not all confer the same sort of power

Valuables confer different rights and capacities before different audiences. A medal recognizes an accomplished act; an object can authorize a future performance or transfer a limited capacity. The right to lend an instrument is not the right to dispose of a community. A king’s apparent embodiment of a whole social order differs again. The capacity attached to an object depends on the claim and its recognition, rather than on an all-purpose substance called wealth. (VA, chapter 4.)

Beads, medicine and royal display acquire powers through particular Malagasy histories, including enslavement. Meaning cannot be inferred from material properties or an undated list of beliefs. Royal attempts to command recognition can fail. Accounts of medicines’ efficacy are actors’ claims and interpretations, not independently observed supernatural events. René’s later claims about lightning and others’ fear of him likewise concern assertion, reputation and power, not witnessed magical murders. (VA, chapters 4 and 7.)

Money can be both a measure of obligations and an object of exchange (DE). Control of violence, information and personal distinction are different powers (DA, jointly authored). These distinctions leave conversion between powers to be explained; they do not establish one substance automatically convertible into every kind of command.

## F04 — Wampum, grief and a relation that cannot be priced

Condolence, names, offices and wampum make social creativity concrete. Political speech cannot simply begin because a procedure says it is time. Grief can obstruct hearing, sight and speech; condolence prepares people to listen and act together. Objects and words help accomplish these transformations rather than decorating an already complete transaction. (VA, chapter 5, L1780–2233.)

Compensation after a death is not the purchase of an equivalent human being. Acknowledging irreplaceable loss and enabling renewed relations differs from declaring the loss fully paid. Recipients’ capacity to refuse matters. A belt can preserve a memory and undertaking without becoming a transferable claim to unlimited repayment. Human economies organize relations around people, but human does not mean humane. (VA, chapter 5; DE, chapters 6–7.)

Captive adoption, torture and coercive demands coexist with condolence and creativity; the latter cannot certify moral innocence. Mourning, councils and political refounding likewise coexist with the historical problem of internal refusal of commands alongside violence against outsiders. One side does not excuse the other. (VA, chapter 5; DA, chapter 11 and conclusion, jointly authored.)

## F05 — Gifts, magic and the limits of debunking

A gift has no single motive independent of its exchange. The original material behind the hau discussion includes a letter, bird-snaring and a cape. An account of movement and creation has to answer to that sequence; a free-floating theory of an object’s spirit cannot replace it. Kwakiutl records also reflect elite positions and colonial disruption, not transparent access to all participants across time. (VA, chapters 6–7, L2234–3645.)

A gift can sustain a relation, release a claim, humiliate someone or make an unpayable obligation explicit. Exact repayment can liberate in one setting and terminate friendship in another. Communism, exchange and hierarchy differentiate relations within situations instead of sorting whole peoples into gift cultures and market cultures. (VA, chapters 6–7; DE, chapter 5.)

Informing people that institutions are human creations does not solve the fetish problem. People can know this and still confront their creations as forces beyond their control. **Recorded revision —** In the hasina discussion, Graeber initially finds an interpretation of sovereign power misleading in relation to his field observations, then revisits nineteenth-century evidence that supports its hierarchical emphasis. The result is a problem of layered, partly incompatible messages, not a victory for his first reading (VA L3324–3382).

Creative illusion remains ambivalent. Ritual or shared fiction can enable conviviality; relentless calculation may be worse. This does not establish a magical explanation’s literal truth or excuse ritual domination. Factual efficacy, participants’ meanings and the relation an enactment creates are different questions; they need not yield the same answer. (VA, chapters 6–7.)
