# Action, recognition and the making of value

Load for valuation, metrics, worthwhile activity, property, gifts or the relation between work and public recognition. Source abbreviations and locator conventions are in the default frameworks module. Headings organize evidence; they do not name a Graeber algorithm.

## F01 — Three meanings that cannot simply be merged

VA, chapters 1–3 (L234–1409), begins with an unsolved relation among moral values, economic value and meaningful difference. Treating all three as preferences does not explain how people come to care about different things; treating all three as symbols can lose what people actually do. The move toward value as the importance of actions attempts to connect these problems, not to discover one underlying price.

The argument proceeds through rival accounts. Rational-choice explanations can redescribe any act as self-interest if the reward includes satisfaction at generosity. That makes the explanation difficult to disconfirm. Structural accounts illuminate relations of difference but can make actors seem merely to reproduce a system they never made. Graeber wants activity, creativity and transformation back in view. His debt to Marx does not require a factory-centered image of valuable work. Making and maintaining people supplies a larger field in which making commodities is one activity.

The imagined whole before which an action matters is not necessarily a bounded, internally consistent society. Someone can act toward several audiences; an audience can misunderstand, exclude or fail to exist as imagined. A discrepancy between a score and an activity is worth investigating, but does not establish fraud. Ask what the score makes visible, to whom, and what recognition enables next. A useful measure may coordinate activity; an exclusive one may confiscate the right to say why it matters.

The later care lecture moves beyond the language of producing people while retaining its analytic usefulness (UT L1992–2000). Value as socially recognized action and care as supporting freedom are related proposals, not interchangeable definitions. When recognition conflicts with a recipient's freedom, the conflict needs a judgment rather than a vocabulary substitution.

## F02 — Recognition can create something, as well as conceal its makers

VA, chapters 3–4 (L850–1779), examines forms through which past activity acquires a visible, enduring expression that mobilizes future action. A token, title or performance can condense a history of work. Its holder may appear to possess intrinsically a capacity other people's activity helped produce. This makes appropriation possible, but does not make every public performer a parasite.

The ethnographic contrasts matter. Baining accounts emphasize feeding and care in making kin; Kayapo naming and ceremonial display give different activities different visibility. Their politics cannot be read off the opposition between invisible nurture and visible spectacle. Public performance and exchange themselves create relations. Yet male authority and women's exclusion can organize which activities receive honor. Neither celebrating care nor abolishing ceremony solves that issue.

An interruption in the Kayapo discussion is particularly valuable: the ideal dual organization of the village sits beside the observation that the communities discussed had not maintained that arrangement since 1936, amid divisions and grudges (VA L1380). The model remains evidence about an imagined order; it cannot substitute for how neighbors actually live. This distinction also applies to a company mission, a family tradition or a group's constitution.

When recognition is contested, reconstruct the work it condenses, its audience, the rights it confers and the actions it encourages next. If no one recognizes the claim, possession may fail. If recognition creates a real common capacity, removing it may destroy something valuable. The conclusion turns on those relations, not a preference for everything unseen over everything visible.

## F03 — Objects do not all confer the same sort of power

VA, chapter 4, moves among valuables, rights, capacities and audiences rather than treating wealth as money. A medal recognizes an accomplished act; an object can authorize a future performance or transfer a limited capacity. The right to lend an instrument is not the right to dispose of a community. A king's apparent embodiment of a whole social order differs again. Identify the particular capacity an object allows someone to claim and whether others accept it.

The Malagasy material traces beads, medicine, royal display and enslavement. Meaning cannot be inferred from material properties or an undated list of beliefs. Royal attempts to command recognition can fail. Accounts of medicines' efficacy are actors' claims and interpretations, not independently observed supernatural events. René's later claims about lightning and others' fear of him likewise concern assertion, reputation and power, not witnessed magical murders (VA chapter 7).

For a contemporary credential, title or asset, ask what actions created it, which claims attached to it, and where those claims cease to work. Calling it imaginary does not make it inconsequential: machinery, recognition and threatened enforcement can give an imagined title material force. Equally, prestige is not always convertible into every other kind of command.

Compare DE's account of money as both a measure of obligations and an object of exchange, and DA's distinction among control of violence, information and personal distinction. These connections permit inquiry into conversion without positing an all-purpose substance called power.

## F04 — Wampum, grief and a relation that cannot be priced

VA, chapter 5 (L1780–2233), makes social creativity concrete through condolence, names, offices and wampum. Political speech cannot simply begin because a procedure says it is time. Grief can obstruct hearing, sight and speech; condolence prepares people to listen and act together. Objects and words help accomplish these transformations rather than decorating an already complete transaction.

Compensation after a death is not the purchase of an equivalent human being. Acknowledging irreplaceable loss and enabling renewed relations differs from declaring the loss fully paid. Recipients' capacity to refuse matters. A belt can preserve a memory and undertaking without becoming a transferable claim to unlimited repayment. DE develops this distinction through human economies, while explicitly refusing to equate human with humane.

The same record includes captive adoption, torture and coercive demands. Condolence and creativity cannot certify moral innocence. DA, chapter 11, jointly returns to mourning, councils and political refounding; its conclusion retains internal refusal of commands alongside violence against outsiders. One side does not excuse the other.

For a present conflict, attend to what makes a proposal hearable before demanding agreement. This does not license copying an Indigenous ceremony into an unrelated setting, claiming entitlement to forgiveness, or assuming symbolic acknowledgment repairs material loss. Establish whether affected people can refuse the repair and what relations they actually want.

## F05 — Gifts, magic and the limits of debunking

VA, chapters 6–7 (L2234–3645), reconstructs particular exchanges instead of assigning every gift one motive. The original material behind the hau discussion includes a letter, bird-snaring and a cape. An account of movement and creation has to answer to that sequence; a free-floating theory of an object's spirit cannot replace it. Kwakiutl records also reflect elite positions and colonial disruption, not transparent access to all participants across time.

A gift can sustain a relation, release a claim, humiliate someone or make an unpayable obligation explicit. Exact repayment can liberate in one setting and terminate friendship in another. DE's communism, exchange and hierarchy differentiate relations within situations instead of sorting whole peoples into gift cultures and market cultures.

Informing people that institutions are human creations does not solve the fetish problem. People can know this and still confront their creations as forces beyond their control. In the hasina discussion, Graeber initially finds an interpretation of sovereign power misleading in relation to his field observations, then revisits nineteenth-century evidence that supports its hierarchical emphasis. The result is a problem of layered, partly incompatible messages, not a victory for his first reading (VA L3324–3382).

Creative illusion remains ambivalent. Ritual or shared fiction can enable conviviality; relentless calculation may be worse. This does not establish a magical explanation's literal truth or excuse ritual domination. Distinguish factual efficacy, participants' meanings and the relation an enactment creates. Where they come apart, preserve the difference instead of choosing credulity or contempt.
