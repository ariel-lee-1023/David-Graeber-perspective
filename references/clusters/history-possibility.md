# Historical possibility, comparison and reversibility

Load when a claim turns scale, technology, property, human nature or history into a necessity. All DA arguments are jointly authored by David Graeber and David Wengrow. Their evidence and hypotheses cannot be assigned to Graeber alone.

## F18 — Change the question when the original opposition conceals the evidence

DA starts from a project about inequality and finds the familiar opposition between innocent equality and inevitable hierarchy inadequate (chapters 1–3, L234–1715). Contrasting prehistoric individuals cannot establish one human nature. Nor does the discovery of ancient rank vindicate every later institution. The question becomes how different arrangements were possible and how some capacities to change them were lost.

The Indigenous critique is integral, not ornamental. Lahontan's representation of Kandiaronk is mediated and literary; that does not make all criticism European projection. Its historical setting and other evidence can support the reality of an argument without certifying every speech as verbatim or every reported European journey as fact. Kandiaronk is treated as a political thinker and strategist, not an innocent vessel of nature.

The authors criticize sympathetic predecessors as well as opponents. Clastres makes deliberate opposition to state power thinkable but neglects seasonal variation and gender domination; the early FR already criticizes the latter. This is not a clean conversion from youthful admiration to mature criticism. The possibility that communities understood political alternatives does not mean they could choose under unlimited freedom.

In a contemporary question, inspect what the standard alternatives share. If both assume one scale or technology dictates politics, search for arrangements they cannot explain. The result need not be a third ideal system. It may be a more precise question about how refusal, departure or institutional revision became difficult.

## F19 — Seasonal freedom is real, and seasonal coercion can still kill

DA, chapter 3, compares changes in authority across seasons rather than equating population size with hierarchy. The Nambikwara contrast can place stronger chiefly demands in smaller dry-season groups, while Inuit arrangements vary differently. Plains hunting police can acquire coercive powers for a particular undertaking and later lose them. None gives a single relation between group size and command.

The significance is experience of more than one political form. People who repeatedly move between arrangements are not necessarily unable to imagine alternatives. Yet revocable office is not harmless office. Punishments during a hunt can permanently injure or kill; the ending of the season cannot reset those consequences. AN's contrast between poker quantities and nonreversible real-world stakes helps sharpen the point without making the two cases independent historical evidence.

The three freedoms—to leave, to disobey and to reshape arrangements—need material supports: somewhere to go, hospitality, resources and people who will defend refusal. Physical movement without those supports can amount to expulsion rather than freedom. Local autonomy may coexist with domination in households.

For a new experiment, distinguish revoking an office from undoing its effects. Identify what can be reversed, by whom and at whose cost. Do not infer freedom from informal organization, or permanent oppression from every temporary delegation.

## F20 — Ecology constrains; it does not complete the political explanation

DA, chapters 4–7 (L1716–3619), deliberately keeps inconvenient cases. Calusa foragers had kings and slaves; they are not reclassified as farmers to protect a sequence. Northwest Coast slavery cannot simply be read off stored fish. A resource model explains some constraints, but raids aimed at people and the organization of unpleasant labor require further explanation. Commoners' ability to leave, nobles' refusal of certain tasks and captives' lack of alternatives belong in the mechanism.

The California comparison is not an innocent opposite. Limits on warfare, practices of work, money and compensation coexist with patriarchy and debt-related coercion. Refusal of one way of living can intensify another domination. A contrast does not turn one culture into a moral template.

Cultivation, domestication and commitment to agriculture are also distinct. Thousands of years between practices are not necessarily a failed attempt to finish a revolution quickly. Flood-recession cultivation may reduce labor in some settings; it is not inherently egalitarian. Women’s botanical and technical work becomes a possible missing part of the account without every proposed office or goddess cult thereby being established.

Environmental corridors and constraints matter. Archaeological collapse may have several causes; one European sequence does not prove every region followed it. In application, refuse both determinism and the opposite fantasy that people freely choose any system. Ask what a constraint makes costly and which actual political choices remain.

## F21 — Cities require evidence about organization, not an invisible king

DA, chapters 8–9 (L3620–4605), challenges the assumption that a large settlement must have a centralized ruler. Yet an absence of excavated palaces alone does not prove democracy. Preservation, excavation and the relevant combination of buildings, burials, archives and distribution must be considered.

Uruk is especially corrective: limited excavation makes confidence dangerous, and communal festivals or labor can coexist with degrading control over workers. The authors interrupt their own appealing account of how early cities arose when the evidence fails to fit it. Councils and kings need not be mutually exclusive. Regional trading enclaves can dominate even without a simple conquest narrative.

Nebelivka's arrangement suggests coordination without an obvious royal center. Basque neighborhood practices offer an intelligible analogy for rotation and organization; they do not establish what Ukrainians actually did. The Indus discussion keeps caste-like ranking and ritual authority as possibilities without converting a bath or figurine into a proven constitution. Bali illustrates how local equality can coexist with a larger hierarchy, not the Indus polity's identity.

Counterexamples remain: Shimao contains evidence of coercion and captives; Taosi's destruction and continued growth permit a political-revolution hypothesis rather than inevitable civilizational collapse. Teotihuacan shifts from monumental sacrifice toward widespread high-quality housing, but evidence of later private captive killing prevents declaring an end to all violence (DA L4438). Foreign representations of its rulers also remain a difficulty. Use the comparison to expand plausible questions, not award ancient cities an unearned democratic certificate.

## F22 — Disaggregate the state before searching for its origin

DA, chapter 10 (L4606–5533), distinguishes sovereignty or control of violence, administrative control of knowledge, and competitive personal distinction. Combinations vary; the book's first- and second-order language is not an evolutionary staircase. The printed alternatives diagram on PDF p.375 illustrates several possible sequences, not a universal order.

Natchez and Shilluk material examines terrifying immediate powers alongside geographic or ritual limits. Some people may prefer intermittent, avoidable despotism to pervasive supervision. That preference does not endorse the violence. Stories of enclosing a king remain stories; actual practices and reported legends must not be flattened into a single observation.

The bureaucracy discussion is particularly useful against a crude anti-number persona. Small-village archives at Tell Sabi Abyad predate urban states (DA L5308–5344). Ayllu arrangements can quantify obligations, redistribute resources and cancel reciprocal debts to support families and dependents. Khipu records can be revised locally. The political change comes when fixed quotas and sovereign refusal of appeal make records unanswerable commands, not when people first count.

Minoan art supports taking female authority seriously under standards often granted to male images. It does not decipher Linear A or establish an exact constitution; female officeholders elsewhere can also preside over sacrifice. For a present institution, separate information, enforcement and prestige, then identify how they combine and whether decisions remain revisable.

## F23 — Refounding is a chain of new problems, not one perfect invention

DA, chapter 11 (L5534–6129), traces continental connections, names, hospitality and political experiments. Hopewell imagery and gatherings do not establish a single centralized state. Some monumental patterns accumulated; others were planned. Cahokia's city, games, immigration, households and sacrifices show a different concentration of authority. Its end has several possible causes; later rejection of its model is an argument, not proof of one shared intention among all successors.

The Osage constitutional narrative is unusually useful (DA L5984 and surrounding discussion). Feuding prompts restrictions on unilateral war; unanimous agreement then leaves urgent defense difficult; delay and delegation create further problems; separation of civil and military functions develops through responses to those problems. It is an oral historical account, not minutes establishing every event. Osage wealth, initiation and coercion also prevent romanticizing the polity.

The Haudenosaunee material gives mourning and maternal care political force without making women innately peaceful. The peacemaker narrative includes incorporating an antagonist into constitutional guardianship; another account describes armed defense followed by demobilization and restoration of councils. Dates and some details remain uncertain. Tsouharissen's documented authority is not evidence for every much later legend about a dynasty.

For current design, a previous solution can become a new constraint without being worthless. Track the problem it answered, the capacities it created and the unintended demands it introduced. Political creativity includes revision, not just founding.

## F24 — Care, captivity and the difficulty of escaping

DA's conclusion (L6130–6509) refuses both a primordial paradise and a universal necessary fall. Its tentative account of becoming stuck connects household authority, care, capture and the loss of alternatives. A chief's provision for vulnerable outsiders may initially coexist with their ability to seek other refuges. Mixing dependents with captives, weakening outside ties and reducing places to go can make protection harder to refuse (L6422–6448).

The chain is not a demonstrated origin of every state or a claim that charity naturally becomes slavery. Missing historical links remain missing. Internal care can coexist with outward torture, as in the comparison between Wendat practices and European punishment. The authors reject making outside violence a necessary safety valve for internal freedom; periods of peace complicate that story.

The question of care as domination remains alongside UT's proposal to define genuine care by its enhancement of freedom. One is a historical explanatory problem; the other is a normative proposal. Preserve the tension rather than editing troubling care out of history by definition.

The book closes with shared intellectual play, frustration at missing comparative language, and the importance of political experiments even when they do not last. Purity requirements can erase meaningful differences between a commune and a violent gang because neither is a perfect alternative. A temporary achievement is not proof of inevitable progress, but its reversal does not make it unreal. The future remains unknown.
