# Historical possibility, comparison and reversibility

**Loading and attribution.** Load when a claim turns scale, technology, property, human nature or history into a necessity. All DA arguments below are jointly authored by David Graeber and David Wengrow; they are not Graeber’s sole-authored discoveries. The archaeological interpretations and hypotheses retain that source status.

## F18 — Change the question when the original opposition conceals the evidence

Innocent equality and inevitable hierarchy are inadequate alternatives for explaining human history. Contrasting prehistoric individuals cannot establish one human nature, and ancient rank cannot vindicate every later institution. The problem is how different arrangements were possible and how some capacities to change them were lost. **Development —** A project initially concerned with inequality shifted toward this question as the familiar opposition failed to accommodate the evidence. (DA, chapters 1–3, L234–1715.)

The Indigenous critique is integral, not ornamental. Lahontan's representation of Kandiaronk is mediated and literary; that does not make all criticism European projection. Its historical setting and other evidence can support the reality of an argument without certifying every speech as verbatim or every reported European journey as fact. Kandiaronk is treated as a political thinker and strategist, not an innocent vessel of nature.

Deliberate opposition to state power is thinkable without assuming communities had unlimited freedom to choose. Clastres’s account opens that possibility but neglects seasonal variation and gender domination. **Chronological qualification —** The early FR already criticizes the gender problem; the later DA criticism is not a clean conversion from youthful admiration to mature rejection.

## F19 — Seasonal freedom is real, and seasonal coercion can still kill

Authority can change across seasons without tracking population size in one direction. Stronger chiefly demands can occur in smaller Nambikwara dry-season groups, while Inuit arrangements vary differently. Plains hunting police can acquire coercive powers for a particular undertaking and later lose them. None establishes a single relation between group size and command. (DA, chapter 3.)

Repeated movement between political arrangements gives people experience of alternatives. Yet revocable office is not harmless office. Punishments during a hunt can permanently injure or kill; the end of the season cannot reset those consequences. **Comparison —** AN’s contrast between poker quantities and nonreversible real-world stakes sharpens the distinction without supplying additional historical evidence for the hunting case.

The three freedoms—to leave, to disobey and to reshape arrangements—need material supports: somewhere to go, hospitality, resources and people who will defend refusal. Physical movement without those supports can amount to expulsion rather than freedom. Local autonomy may coexist with domination in households.

Revocation of an office and reversal of its effects are different possibilities. Temporary delegation does not itself establish permanent domination, but its temporary character cannot undo injury. The scope of authority and the consequences of its exercise remain separate questions. (DA, chapter 3; AN, play and games.)

## F20 — Ecology constrains; it does not complete the political explanation

Ecology alone cannot complete a political explanation. Calusa foragers had kings and slaves; reclassifying them as farmers would preserve a sequence by excluding its counterexample. Northwest Coast slavery cannot simply be read off stored fish. Raids aimed at people and the organization of unpleasant labor require further explanation. Commoners’ ability to leave, nobles’ refusal of certain tasks and captives’ lack of alternatives belong in the mechanism. (DA, chapters 4–7, L1716–3619.)

The California comparison is not an innocent opposite. Limits on warfare, practices of work, money and compensation coexist with patriarchy and debt-related coercion. Refusal of one way of living can intensify another domination. A contrast does not turn one culture into a moral template.

Cultivation, domestication and commitment to agriculture are also distinct. Thousands of years between practices are not necessarily a failed attempt to finish a revolution quickly. Flood-recession cultivation may reduce labor in some settings; it is not inherently egalitarian. Women’s botanical and technical work becomes a possible missing part of the account without every proposed office or goddess cult thereby being established.

Environmental corridors and constraints matter; they do not determine every political arrangement. Archaeological collapse may have several causes, and one European sequence does not establish what happened in every region. Material constraints and political choices belong in the same explanation. (DA, chapters 4–7.)

## F21 — Cities require evidence about organization, not an invisible king

A large settlement does not necessarily imply a centralized ruler. Yet an absence of excavated palaces alone does not prove democracy. Preservation, excavation and the relevant combination of buildings, burials, archives and distribution constrain what can be inferred. (DA, chapters 8–9, L3620–4605.)

Uruk’s limited excavation makes confidence dangerous, and communal festivals or labor can coexist with degrading control over workers. Councils and kings need not be mutually exclusive. Regional trading enclaves can dominate without a simple conquest narrative. **Recorded revision —** The authors interrupt their own appealing account of how early cities arose when the evidence fails to fit it. (DA, chapters 8–9.)

Nebelivka’s arrangement suggests coordination without an obvious royal center. Basque neighborhood practices offer an intelligible analogy for rotation and organization; they do not establish what Ukrainians actually did. Caste-like ranking and ritual authority remain possibilities in the Indus case without a bath or figurine establishing a constitution. Bali illustrates how local equality can coexist with a larger hierarchy, not the identity of the Indus polity. (DA, chapters 8–9.)

Counterexamples remain: Shimao contains evidence of coercion and captives; Taosi’s destruction and continued growth permit a political-revolution hypothesis rather than inevitable civilizational collapse. Teotihuacan shifts from monumental sacrifice toward widespread high-quality housing, but evidence of later private captive killing prevents declaring an end to all violence (DA L4438). Foreign representations of its rulers also remain a difficulty. These changes expand the possible account of urban organization without establishing an unqualified democratic constitution.

## F22 — Disaggregate the state before searching for its origin

Control of violence, administrative control of knowledge and competitive personal distinction are distinct bases of power. Their combinations vary. First- and second-order regimes name combinations, not steps on a universal evolutionary staircase. **Source note —** DA, chapter 10 (L4606–5533), and its alternatives diagram on PDF p.375 present several possible sequences.

Terrifying immediate powers coexist with geographic or ritual limits in the Natchez and Shilluk cases. Some people may prefer intermittent, avoidable despotism to pervasive supervision; that preference does not vindicate the violence. Stories of enclosing a king remain stories, distinct from observed practices. (DA, chapter 10.)

Small-village archives at Tell Sabi Abyad predate urban states. Ayllu arrangements can quantify obligations, redistribute resources and cancel reciprocal debts to support families and dependents. Khipu records can be revised locally. The political danger arises when equalizing measurement is joined to sovereignty and becomes an unanswerable command; counting alone does not explain that transformation. An analogy between village administrations does not establish that Mesopotamia used Andean institutions. (DA L5308–5344.)

Minoan art supports taking female authority seriously under standards often granted to male images. It does not decipher Linear A or establish an exact constitution; female officeholders elsewhere can also preside over sacrifice. Representation, office and the character of rule remain different questions. (DA, chapter 10.)

## F23 — Refounding is a chain of new problems, not one perfect invention

Continental connections, names and hospitality create possibilities for political experiments without establishing one continental polity. Hopewell imagery and gatherings do not prove a centralized state. Some monumental patterns accumulated; others were planned. Cahokia’s city, games, immigration, households and sacrifices show a different concentration of authority. Its end has several possible causes; later rejection of its model is an argument, not proof of one shared intention among all successors. (DA, chapter 11, L5534–6129.)

**Constitutional sequence —** Feuding prompts restrictions on unilateral war; unanimous agreement then leaves urgent defense difficult; delay and delegation create further problems; separation of civil and military functions develops through responses to those problems. This Osage account is an oral historical narrative, not minutes establishing every event. Osage wealth, initiation and coercion also limit any picture of an uncomplicated free polity. (DA L5984 and surrounding discussion.)

Mourning and maternal care can acquire political force without making women innately peaceful. The Haudenosaunee peacemaker narrative includes incorporating an antagonist into constitutional guardianship; another account describes armed defense followed by demobilization and restoration of councils. Dates and some details remain uncertain. Tsouharissen’s documented authority is not evidence for every much later legend about a dynasty. (DA, chapter 11.)

A constitutional solution can create a further practical problem. In the Osage sequence, restraints on unilateral war protect the group from one danger while obstructing urgent defense; subsequent changes answer the new difficulty. Political invention includes revision of working arrangements, not only an original founding. (DA, chapter 11.)

## F24 — Care, captivity and the difficulty of escaping

Neither a primordial paradise nor a universal necessary fall explains becoming stuck in domination. A tentative alternative connects household authority, care, capture and the loss of exits. A chief’s provision for vulnerable outsiders may initially coexist with their ability to seek other refuges. Mixing dependents with captives, weakening outside ties and reducing places to go can make protection harder to refuse. This is a historical hypothesis, not a demonstrated universal sequence. (DA, conclusion, L6130–6509; especially L6422–6448.)

The chain does not establish the origin of every state or imply that charity naturally becomes slavery. Missing historical links remain missing. Internal care can coexist with outward torture, as in the comparison between Wendat practices and European punishment. External violence need not be the necessary safety valve for internal freedom; periods of peace complicate that explanation. (DA, conclusion.)

Care as a possible relation of domination remains in tension with care defined by its enhancement of freedom. The former is a historical explanatory problem in DA; the latter is a normative proposal in UT. A definition of genuine care cannot remove troubling caring relationships from the historical record.

Political experiments can matter even when they do not last. A purity requirement can erase meaningful differences between a commune and a violent gang because neither is a perfect alternative. A temporary achievement is not proof of inevitable progress, but its reversal does not make it unreal. The future remains unknown. **Closing register —** Shared intellectual play and frustration at missing comparative language coexist in DA’s closing discussion.
