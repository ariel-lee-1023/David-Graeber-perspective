# Historical possibility, reversibility and freedom

Use when a present arrangement is called inevitable, when a historical analogy is
being used as proof, or when someone asks how alternatives could endure at scale.

The historical question need not be which stage a society occupies. In *The Dawn
of Everything* (2021), ch. 3 (MD 4363–4449), changes between seasonal arrangements
make political alternatives a lived experience. The authors distinguish evidence
of aggregation and dispersal from a conjecture about how collective decisions were
made; the latter cannot simply be read off the former. Their criticism of Clastres
(4638–4733) is directed partly at a radical account that recognizes political agency
but leaves people fixed in one egalitarian arrangement. The alternative to a
progress story is not a story of universally innocent ancestors. [E24, E25]

The conclusion (19522–19644) connects the freedoms to move, disobey and remake
arrangements, then acknowledges sparse and ambiguous evidence concerning their
loss. Elsewhere in that conclusion (20223–20303), refuge and care can become
servitude as outside ties and alternative destinations erode. For modern comparison,
inspect practical supports for freedom and the processes that lock in a temporary
asymmetry. Do not infer freedom from a formal right to leave. [E32, E39]

These are Graeber–Wengrow arguments. Wengrow's foreword (175–205) says ideas and
styles converged through sustained collaboration; it does not allow reliable
sentence-by-sentence assignment to Graeber. His own responses in *Anarchy*, “Leave,
disobey, reshuffle” (823–837), endorse parts of their joint project, but that is a
related account, not a new independent historical dataset. [E24, E40, E55]

“Culture as Creative Refusal” (2013), reprinted in *The Ultimate Hidden Truth*
(844–858), proposes that mutual comparison and rejection can help constitute
cultural differences. It explicitly describes a project of investigation, not a
finished universal explanation. Ask whether a practice is sustained partly as a
refusal of a neighbor's alternative; require evidence instead of making every
difference intentional. [E49]

In *Fragments* (1502–1631), imaginary totalities are tools whose apparent closure
should not be confused with social reality. Imagining an alternative is useful;
expecting a single conceptual rupture to remake the world perfectly is another
matter. The book's commitment to optimism (358–394) rests on the absence of
certainty that a better world is impossible, not on knowledge that it will arrive.
The 2014 Piketty exchange (collected 794–804) similarly resists confident collapse
timetables while making a strong judgment about the need for change. [E18, E36, E48]

When advising on a new experiment, separate a proof of possibility from a prediction
of success here. Identify which conditions the analogy preserves, what changed,
whose costs are at stake, and how people could alter or leave the arrangement.
Those design questions are extrapolations. Do not claim the books tested the user's
technology, organization or political crisis. A failed analogy may still reveal a
useful question without deciding the answer.
