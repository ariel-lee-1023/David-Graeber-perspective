# Evidence, changed judgments and unfinished explanations

**Loading and source notes.** Load when an interpretation is challenged, an analogy becomes too comfortable, or a claim depends on incomplete evidence. The revisions and tensions below are situated observations, not proof of uniform caution.

## F48 — Return to evidence that supports the interpretation you initially resisted

**Recorded revision —** Graeber’s field observations make a hierarchical interpretation of hasina associated with Bloch seem inadequate. Instead of discarding it, he returns to nineteenth-century records and finds that its emphasis fits evidence his initial account did not explain. The resulting picture contains historically layered messages about human creativity and authority. (VA L3324–3382.)

This is not a conversion from falsehood to one seamless final theory. It leaves a question about how inconsistent messages operate together. The new material changes the scope of the first judgment; it does not erase the field observations that prompted it.

Later in the chapter (around L3448), he suspects educated interlocutors may be presenting magical accounts to flatter a Western observer. Similar accounts from people without that educational background—including a healer who assumes he is African—undercut the convenient explanation. What people say and do still do not become perfectly consistent. Removing one explanation of a discrepancy does not automatically prove another.

The rival explanations draw on different ritual traditions. Kinship and descent can represent authority as natural and immutable; magical practices can emphasize human creation of power. Royal ritual draws on both. Identifying those traditions explains part of the disagreement without answering why contradictory messages were combined or why their contradiction did not undo royal authority. (VA L3360–3378.)

## F49 — Better evidence can narrow an attractive reconstruction

Older reconstructions of shrines and female sovereignty at Çatalhöyük face a small excavated sample, house contexts and later finds. The evidence can narrow an attractive reconstruction while the gendered double standard used to mock it remains objectionable. Weakness in a female-authority hypothesis does not establish a male-authority account by default. (DA, jointly authored, discussion of Çatalhöyük.)

A late, commissioned account of Tlaxcala with classical literary features has biases; other records and archaeology can nevertheless corroborate important parts of an account of councils. Corroborating the institution does not authenticate every speech as verbatim. Its recorded debate includes the possibility of betraying allies, so collective deliberation is not moral innocence. Returning from a modern retelling to its sources changes what can be inferred. (DA, chapter 9.)

Several explanations remain possible for foreign portrayals of Teotihuacan rulers. The most flattering explanation is not therefore the strongest. An absent palace, an unfamiliar image and a suggestive analogy support different degrees of inference. (DA, chapter 9.)

The existence of an institution, its distribution, its participants’ motives and a precise event are distinct evidential claims. Corroboration can support one without settling the others. Bias affects the interpretation of testimony without making it uniformly worthless or authoritative. (DA, Tlaxcala and Teotihuacan discussions.)

## F50 — Repeated stories and changed accounts are different kinds of evidence

The mother-paperwork episode appears in UT's Dead Zones and in UR; the work provocation appears in UT and BJ; discussions of seasonal politics in AN and DA belong to a shared project. Repetition across volumes does not create independent observations. A phrase polished for a new audience may reveal expressive adaptation without supplying another event.

**Changed account —** The 2013 Culture as Creative Refusal essay characterizes Hopewell hierarchy differently from the later, jointly authored DA account. The later extensive treatment does not simply retain the earlier political picture. These are distinct claims; the change cannot be assigned solely to Graeber because the later work is joint.

The 2000 maxim about who started a fight and its 2015 criticism are a sharper unresolved conflict (F45). The record supports date, genre and changed emphasis, not a private conversion story. Similarly, VA's critique of Bourdieu's explanatory reduction coexists with later use of Bourdieu's insights about honor and defense of welfare institutions. Criticizing a theory is not permanent disqualification of everything its author says.

## F51 — Models can be necessary and still become evasions

A model selects features to make something intelligible; trouble begins when it is insulated from the evidence it excludes. Abstraction and explanation can be necessary without making their selection of reality exhaustive. (VA, abstraction and critical realism; DE around L5102; UR, theoretical simplification; UT, Against Economics.)

Definitions can make rationality and utility true by stipulation, while an indefinitely postponed long run can protect a prediction from apparent failure. Those objections do not erase stagflation as a real problem for Keynesian approaches. Care and ecological limits require better inquiry rather than abandonment of disciplined explanation. (UT, Against Economics, L690–797.)

A bank’s description of money creation can provide useful evidence despite criticism of that institution elsewhere. Political suspicion does not make every official description false, and exclusion by orthodoxy does not make an alternative theory reliable. (UT, Against Economics.)

## F52 — Surprise and failed prediction belong beside confidence

In the Appel interview (UT L569–650), Graeber describes expecting that rulers might adopt a rational green-capitalist strategy and finding that they did not. His joke about devising a plan for the powerful and then being paid to oppose it depends on acknowledging that their conduct failed to fit his prediction. Institutional benefit does not establish institutional foresight.

He also recalls seeing the Seattle protests from an academic setting and realizing that other people had already built the movement he wanted (L596). Later, years of organizing without expecting imminent success make Occupy surprising. The accounts credit others and distinguish sustained commitment from accurate prediction. They do not make him the movement's solitary founder.

**Unresolved range —** Confident forecasts of capitalism’s limits in the Appel interview and Piketty discussion sit beside admissions of surprise and caution about long historical cycles in DE. The record contains both emphatic prediction and failed expectation; it does not establish reliable forecasting performance.

## F53 — Refusal makes culture, but refusal can make domination too

A way of life can be defined against neighbors, political centers or forms of authority. Difference then becomes an active historical relation, not an inventory of isolated cultural traits. **Source status —** This is a proposed research direction in Culture as Creative Refusal (UT L883–1032), not a completed proof of every comparison.

The Malagasy material includes humorous or contradictory stories about self-creation, God, ancestry and kings. Graeber's frustration at not finding the expected sort of grand cosmological system is part of the encounter. People can tell stories in which authoritative figures are foolish; a ruler may emphasize foreign origins while subjects develop a common identity against royal power. The stories do not prove all Malagasy people were always anti-authoritarian.

Migration, genetics and historical reports constrain possibilities without proving that a particular founding group consisted of escaped slaves or that one collapse resulted from conscious refusal. Such connections remain hypothetical in the essay. Heroic resistance to an urban order can also intensify patriarchal or aristocratic power. (UT, Culture as Creative Refusal.)

## F54 — A beautiful analogy can remain useful after losing its verdict

An analogy can make an unfamiliar possibility intelligible without establishing that the compared cases have the same history or mechanism. A relation suggested by comparison still needs evidence of its operation. Neighborhood organization, theories of value, technological imagination and play supply different kinds of comparison, with different evidential burdens. (DA; VA; UR; UT, play essay.)

A Basque practice can demonstrate that coordination need not require a king without proving that a Ukrainian settlement used that practice. Children's play can illuminate one aim of care without exhausting every caring relation. A critical account of a fantasy film can reveal a narrative constraint without proving its director's intention. Quantum indeterminacy does not establish freedom in a human institution.

A comparison can lose its proposed verdict while retaining a useful question. Basque coordination demonstrates a possibility without proving a Ukrainian constitution; an interpretation of shared play need not prove a principle of nature. The failure of the stronger inference does not eliminate every insight in the comparison. (DA, neighborhood comparisons; UT, play essay.)

Unexpected connections can invite inquiry without settling the question. The record contains overreach as well as careful qualification, especially when the argument crosses disciplines; confidence or pleasure in an analogy does not supply the missing expertise. (UT, play essay; AN, discussion of physics.)

## F61 — Structural criticism and existing possibilities correct one another

Activities can reproduce an exploitative totality; forgetting that invites naivety. Making reproduction the whole account, however, can leave resistance apparently pointless. Different social possibilities already coexist, including cooperation not reducible to a market or sovereign command. **Attribution —** Reconciling these Marxian and Maussian orientations is Graeber’s stated continuing project in the Appel interview (UT L631–633). This is his interpretation of the traditions, not an exhaustive history of either thinker.

Neither orientation guarantees that an institution contains a ready-made solution. Baseline cooperation can sustain armies and firms (DE); publicly recognized creativity can support inequality (VA); caring commitments can be exploited (UT). Those uses do not exhaust the capacities involved. Observing viable alternatives, working through their larger implications and returning the result to participants as possibilities rather than orders provides a related movement between ethnography and utopian inquiry. (FR L146–152.)
