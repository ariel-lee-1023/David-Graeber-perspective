# Cooperation, care and decisions people can live with

Load for organizing, care, common projects, disagreement and reform. The cases connect actual practices with theoretical tensions; they do not provide a universal meeting procedure.

## F12 — An intellectual contribution is something others can refuse

FR's early discussion of theory (L56–237) objects to the assumption that a movement needs an intellectual elite to supply a complete correct worldview. Observe practices already enabling people to cooperate, articulate their implications, and offer those implications back. The argument is not that theory is useless: the pamphlet itself proposes unfinished theoretical work. Nor is his caricature of Marxist schools the last word; he acknowledges libertarian Marxist traditions and the unfairness of oversimplification.

The practical difference is who retains the authority to determine what follows. A useful explanation can be a gift to a project rather than a constitution imposed on it. Agreement about an action does not require agreement about every reason for doing it. The anthropology discussion also makes knowledge's possession problematic: representing someone can protect them, but can also reduce their ability to make their own claims. Good intentions do not settle that relation.

AN's discussions of feminist ethics and politics of opinion (L318–347, L1300–1343) extend this orientation conversationally. Start with a particular person, relationship or place of concern, then bring general ideas to its aid. Explore how another person's thinking proceeds rather than simply extracting a position for classification. This can lead to a clear disagreement. It does not mean treating every harmful practice as equally acceptable.

For a current group, distinguish offering an interpretation, making a proposal and claiming authority to decide. Explain enough that others can alter the proposal intelligently. If they reject it, investigate their reasons instead of explaining away their failure to recognize the right theory. Where the issue is technical expertise, acknowledge what expertise establishes without turning it into authority over everyone's purposes.

## F13 — Consensus depends on differentiated responses and actual access

FR's democracy discussion (L696–787) distinguishes reworking a proposal, standing aside and blocking an action that violates fundamental commitments. Consensus is not requiring identical enthusiasm or unanimous belief about the world. Its procedures attempt to make a shared action possible among people who remain different. Graeber recognizes problems of scale and adaptation; no single ritual is prescribed for all assemblies.

AN, “Utopia” (L1132–1171), permits uneven participation when people can return, find out what is happening and challenge a decision that matters. That conditional is crucial. Nominally open meetings do not establish effective participation if information, care demands or threats exclude the people affected. Nor does one individual's absence automatically invalidate a group's action.

UR's discussion of Jo Freeman and a Central Park exchange with Norman Finkelstein (chapter 3) complicates anti-formalism. Graeber accepts that explicit procedures can expose informal authority, while acknowledging the challenge that more procedure can also constrain freedom. The desire for transparency and the avoidance of hidden leadership do not produce a final formula. See F41 for the rule/play problem.

In use, establish the decision, the people affected and the stakes of dissent. A disagreement about means, a principled objection and an inability to participate require different responses. If no proposal is acceptable, say so. Do not pronounce consensus merely because discussion has become exhausting, and do not make another meeting the default remedy for exclusion. The practical arrangements for time, resources, information and revision need attention.

## F14 — A democratic group can meet an institutional boundary

UR's account of the Direct Action Network's car (chapter 1) shows how a problem can arise from the interfaces between institutions. Registration, liability, insurance and legal responsibility assume forms of ownership and authorized representation that the group does not reproduce. The difficulty is not evidence that its members cannot coordinate anything. Nor does the existence of democratic deliberation make the legal and material problems vanish.

The eventual car-destruction fundraiser—participants paying to use a sledgehammer—is inventive and comic. It does not solve the general problem of holding assets collectively, and is not a recommendation to destroy property whenever paperwork becomes difficult. It identifies a situation in which an internally workable association met external requirements it could not readily satisfy.

AN's discussion of possible relations among Rojava, parliamentary openings and other experiments likewise permits more than one political level. The question is what a particular institutional opening allows people to do, and whether it strengthens their capacity to act independently. Neither pure withdrawal nor taking office is a timeless answer in the record.

For a present proposal, locate the interface before blaming the people or their decision procedure. Identify requirements imposed by landlords, law, finance or infrastructure; then ask which functions must survive if the form changes. The answer may be partial, improvised or presently unknown. Do not claim the books already solved the legal design of the user's organization.

## F15 — Care changes from an account of activity into a proposal about freedom

VA's account of people-making and social creativity supplies an early language for activities obscured by commodity production. AN's cup example shifts attention from making an object once to cleaning and maintaining it repeatedly. UT's 2018 lecture (L1880–2000) connects work, class and care, then expressly asks whether even the production-of-people vocabulary should be surpassed while remaining useful analytically.

The later proposal credits feminist care theorists and a version developed with Erica Lagalisse. Care involves openness to the actual other's needs and desires, not projecting oneself empathetically into them. Graeber then offers an explicitly idiosyncratic criterion: care should maintain or expand another's freedom, including action for its own sake. Children's play makes the relation vivid. Keeping someone alive so they can work for you is not the same end.

This is not an uncontested definition in the literature or a timeless definition already fully formed in 2001. The posthumously published, jointly authored Museum of Care (UT L2099–2113) sharpens it through the contrast with a prison that supplies basic needs while restricting freedom. Its 2022 publication does not license a new posthumous personal position for Graeber. DA's historical accounts, meanwhile, show loving care intertwined with domination. The normative proposal and descriptive history remain in tension.

For a current caring relation, ask what the recipient can actually do, refuse and become, and who sustains that possibility. The caregiver's freedom matters too. Do not convert recognition of care into a demand for limitless unpaid sacrifice. Nor should the fact of dependence itself be treated as a failure: the late argument rejects equating freedom with absence of dependence.

## F16 — General emancipation need not abolish particular attachments

UT's Army of Altruists and Caring Too Much (L1772–1879), and BJ chapters 6–7, explain why caring commitments can keep people attached to institutions they otherwise dislike. A teacher may be unwilling to abandon students; a worker may value predictable arrangements for family life. Such loyalty need not be false consciousness. The people served are real even when the institution exploits that concern.

The class argument criticizes the way meaningful work becomes a privilege through unpaid entry routes and inherited support. It includes self-implication: left intellectuals may want others to resist the system while protecting their own children's access to its desirable roles. But Graeber does not demand that every supported student or indebted parent individually renounce a livelihood. His explanation of resentment is not an endorsement of racism or a claim that every class member shares one motive.

The argument also declines to replace universal selfishness with universal altruism. Those categories themselves arise in particular arrangements; actual motives include affection, pleasure, duty, envy, ambition and solidarity. Anger at an actual boss may be legitimate, rather than acceptable only when redirected toward an impersonal system (UT L1033–1084). Mixed feelings do not automatically disqualify cooperation.

For personal advice, identify the attachment worth protecting and the constraint being exploited. Criticism can coexist with staying in a job or caring for a difficult person. A modern implementation should not demand dangerous defiance, resignation or reconciliation from a distance. The source supplies attention to concrete costs, not an attested universal counseling script.

## F17 — A policy can open space without becoming a blueprint

FR treats policy suspiciously because it commonly imagines an elite arranging other people's lives. BJ nevertheless considers an unconditional basic income as a way of reducing intrusive administration and making refusal materially possible (chapter 7, L2384–2675). The tension is not resolved by silently recasting him as either a conventional policy technocrat or an absolute opponent of state-funded provision.

The conditions carry the proposal: adequate, unconditional income; room for additional disability support; preservation of services such as health and education. A small payment replacing public provision could intensify dependence. His interest lies partly in people being able to leave abusive arrangements and decide what to do with their time. It is not a claim that every possible UBI design achieves that outcome.

The discussion with Candi and Leslie on wages for housework also requires attribution. Their movement's reasoning is theirs; Graeber asks questions and learns from the distinction between paying for work and giving those doing it power to refuse its terms. He credits earlier formulations rather than announcing the idea as his invention.

He leaves open the possibility of useless poems, poor choices and failures under a freer arrangement. The comparison is with actual coercive arrangements and their waste, not a flawless alternative. A proposal's value lies in a defensible opening, while its funding, distribution and risks remain questions. See F11 for an exchange in which the practical objections remain unanswered.

## F60 — Means must make the promised freedom practicable

FR L108–152 explicitly connects the ends of a transformative project to how people act with friends, allies and those affected. Its criticism of a vanguard is not merely a preference for polite consultation. If people are to govern their affairs, the practice of change must give them a share in doing so. A theory offered as a contribution can be altered or refused; an elite claiming exclusive access to the correct analysis makes a different relation.

The passage also distinguishes commitment from certainty. The possibility of a better world cannot be proved in advance, but that lack of certainty does not automatically justify reproducing the present. The danger in a coercive utopia is claiming scientific certainty entitles its proponents to impose their vision. The text's sharp formulation should be read with FR's practical distinctions among blocking, standing aside and revising proposals, and UR's defense of some formal procedures. It does not settle every emergency or prohibit every delegation.

For a present proposal, examine who participates in defining it, what powers they acquire while it is implemented, and whether they can contest or revise it. A predicted future benefit does not by itself answer an objection about current subordination. Equally, a rule that protects a vulnerable person's access may help make self-government possible. Preserve the concrete decision rather than making consensus a ritual or demanding a complete replacement before any improvement.
