# Cooperation, care and decisions people can live with

**Loading and source notes.** Load for organizing, care, common projects, disagreement and reform. The cases connect actual practices with theoretical tensions; they do not establish a universal meeting procedure.

## F12 — An intellectual contribution is something others can refuse

A movement need not receive a complete correct worldview from an intellectual elite. An ethnographic contribution begins by observing practices already enabling cooperation, articulates their implications, and offers those implications back to participants. Theory remains necessary, but unfinished and open to refusal. **Source qualification —** The contrast between anarchist practice and Marxist schools is expressly qualified by recognition of libertarian Marxist traditions and the unfairness of caricature. (FR L56–237.)

The practical difference is who retains the authority to determine what follows. A useful explanation can be a gift to a project rather than a constitution imposed on it. Agreement about an action does not require agreement about every reason for doing it. The anthropology discussion also makes knowledge's possession problematic: representing someone can protect them, but can also reduce their ability to make their own claims. Good intentions do not settle that relation.

General ideas can begin in care for a particular person, relationship or place and serve the effort to understand it. Following another person’s premises is different from extracting a position for classification. This can lead to clear disagreement; attention to the particular does not make every harmful practice equally acceptable. (AN, feminist ethics and politics of opinion, L318–347, L1300–1343; DG’s contributions remain distinct from other speakers’.)

## F13 — Consensus depends on differentiated responses and actual access

Reworking a proposal, standing aside and blocking an action that violates fundamental commitments are different responses. Consensus does not require identical enthusiasm or unanimous belief about the world. Its procedures attempt to make a shared action possible among people who remain different. Scale and adaptation remain problems; no single ritual fits all assemblies. (FR, democracy discussion, L696–787.)

Uneven participation need not defeat a group’s autonomy when people can return, find out what is happening and challenge a decision that matters. The possibility of re-entry and challenge is a substantive condition, not an incidental concession. One individual’s absence does not automatically invalidate a group’s action. (AN, “Utopia,” L1132–1171.)

Explicit procedures can expose informal authority; more procedure can also constrain freedom. **Recorded encounter —** Jo Freeman’s criticism of hidden leadership and the Central Park exchange with Norman Finkelstein complicate Graeber’s anti-formalism. He accepts the force of the challenge without supplying a final formula for transparency and leadership. (UR, chapter 3; see F41.)

## F14 — A democratic group can meet an institutional boundary

**Recorded institutional encounter —** Registration, liability, insurance and legal responsibility assume forms of ownership and authorized representation that the Direct Action Network does not reproduce. Its car creates a problem at the interface between institutions. This is not evidence that its members cannot coordinate, but democratic deliberation alone does not make the external legal and material requirements disappear. (UR, chapter 1.)

The eventual car-destruction fundraiser—participants paying to use a sledgehammer—is inventive and comic. It does not solve the general problem of holding assets collectively, and is not a recommendation to destroy property whenever paperwork becomes difficult. It identifies a situation in which an internally workable association met external requirements it could not readily satisfy.

Rojava, parliamentary openings and other experiments can involve more than one political level. A particular institutional opening matters for what it allows people to do and whether it strengthens their capacity to act independently. Neither pure withdrawal nor taking office supplies a timeless answer. (AN, discussion of political experiments.)

## F15 — Care changes from an account of activity into a proposal about freedom

**Development across sources —** People-making and social creativity name activities obscured by commodity production (VA). Making an object once is different from cleaning and maintaining it repeatedly, as in the cup example (AN). Work, class and care lead toward a further question: whether even the production-of-people vocabulary should be surpassed while remaining useful analytically. (UT, 2018 lecture, L1880–2000.)

Care involves openness to the actual other’s needs and desires, rather than projecting oneself empathetically into them. A further proposed criterion is that care should maintain or expand another’s freedom, including action for its own sake. Children’s play makes the relation vivid. Keeping someone alive so they can work for you is not the same end. **Attribution —** The account credits feminist care theorists and a version developed with Erica Lagalisse; the freedom criterion is offered as Graeber’s explicitly idiosyncratic proposal. (UT L1992–2000.)

A prison can supply basic needs while restricting freedom. Sustaining life therefore does not by itself meet the proposed care-as-freedom criterion. **Attribution and development —** This contrast appears in the jointly authored, posthumously published Museum of Care (UT L2099–2113); its 2022 publication does not establish a personal 2022 revision by Graeber. The criterion is neither an uncontested definition nor one already fully formed in 2001. DA’s historical accounts of loving care intertwined with domination remain in tension with this normative proposal.

Dependence is not inherently opposed to freedom. Caring activity can make another’s freedom possible rather than replace it with the caregiver’s preferred ends. Its value cannot be identified simply with keeping the recipient productive. (UT, 2018 care lecture.)

## F16 — General emancipation need not abolish particular attachments

Caring commitments can keep people attached to institutions they otherwise dislike. A teacher may be unwilling to abandon students; a worker may value predictable arrangements for family life. Such loyalty need not be false consciousness. The people served are real even when the institution exploits that concern. (UT, Army of Altruists and Caring Too Much, L1772–1879; BJ, chapters 6–7.)

Unpaid entry routes and inherited support can make meaningful work a privilege. A contradiction follows when left intellectuals want others to resist the system while protecting their own children’s access to its desirable roles. Structural criticism does not by itself require every supported student or indebted parent to renounce a livelihood. An explanation of resentment is not an endorsement of racism or a claim that every class member shares one motive. (UT, Army of Altruists and Caring Too Much.)

Universal altruism is no adequate replacement for universal selfishness. Those categories themselves arise in particular arrangements; actual motives include affection, pleasure, duty, envy, ambition and solidarity. Anger at an actual boss may be legitimate, rather than acceptable only when redirected toward an impersonal system. Mixed feelings do not automatically disqualify cooperation. (UT L1033–1084, L1772–1879.)

## F17 — A policy can open space without becoming a blueprint

“Policy” can presume an elite arranging other people’s lives (FR). An unconditional basic income can nevertheless reduce intrusive administration and make refusal materially possible (BJ, chapter 7, L2384–2675). Suspicion of policy and support for this provision remain in tension; neither a general endorsement of technocracy nor an absolute rejection of state-funded provision captures both positions.

The conditions carry the income proposal: adequate, unconditional income; room for additional disability support; preservation of services such as health and education. A small payment replacing public provision could intensify dependence. Freedom to leave abusive arrangements and decide what to do with one’s time is part of its rationale, not a guaranteed result of every possible UBI design. (BJ, chapter 7.)

**Recorded exchange —** In the discussion with Candi and Leslie on wages for housework, their movement’s reasoning remains theirs. Graeber asks questions and learns from the distinction between paying for work and giving those doing it power to refuse its terms. He credits earlier formulations rather than announcing the idea as his invention. (BJ, chapter 7.)

A freer arrangement leaves room for useless poems, poor choices and failures. The comparison is with actual coercive arrangements and their waste, not a flawless alternative. A defensible opening does not settle a proposal’s funding, distribution or risks. (BJ, chapter 7; see F11 for the unresolved practical objections in the Piketty exchange.)

## F60 — Means must make the promised freedom practicable

The means of a transformative project must give participants a practical share in the freedom it promises. Relationships with friends, allies and those affected already constitute part of that change. This criticism of a vanguard is not merely a preference for polite consultation: a theory offered as a contribution can be altered or refused, while an elite claiming exclusive access to the correct analysis establishes a different relation. (FR L108–152.)

Commitment to a better world does not require certainty that it will succeed. Its possibility cannot be proved in advance, but uncertainty does not automatically justify reproducing the present. Claiming scientific certainty becomes dangerous when it licenses imposing a utopia. **Scope of the argument —** FR’s practical distinctions among blocking, standing aside and revising proposals, and UR’s defense of some formal procedures, leave room for situated judgment; they do not settle every emergency or prohibit every delegation. (FR L108–152; F13, F39–F41.)
